Research Overview — Vertical Federated Learning Security

Detecting Active Label Inference Attacks via the Fisher Class-Separability Divergence

In Vertical Federated Learning (VFL), a malicious active party can amplify gradients to silently leak the passive party's labels — with no detectable change in task accuracy. This document explains, from first principles, how monitoring the asymmetry in class-separability between party embeddings — quantified by the Fisher criterion — enables real-time detection and mitigation of this attack. All charts use real experimental measurements from CIFAR-10 training runs.

§1

Motivation — Why This Problem Matters

Core threat: A training partner in a VFL system can recover the other party's private labels without modifying predictions, raising no accuracy alarms, and leaving no trace in task metrics.

Vertical Federated Learning lets two organisations jointly train a model on complementary feature sets without exchanging raw data. In a typical deployment: a hospital holds patient features, a clinical partner holds diagnosis labels, and a shared top-model learns from both. The protocol is designed to keep each party's data private.

The problem is gradients. During back-propagation, each party receives gradient signals from the shared model. Those gradients carry information about the other party's data — including their labels — and a malicious party can amplify this signal without anyone noticing.

Why existing defences fall short

  • Differential privacy adds too much noise to stay useful at reasonable privacy budgets
  • Gradient compression (Top-k, random sparsification) reduces leakage but does not detect attacks
  • Accuracy monitoring cannot flag the attack — task performance is deliberately preserved
  • Laplacian noise perturbation is ineffective against amplification-based attackers

Why Fisher Divergence?

  • The attack leaves a geometric footprint: Party A's embeddings become unusually class-discriminative
  • The signal is relative — comparing Party A vs B — so normal learning variation cancels out
  • Computation is cheap (one forward pass over stored embeddings per epoch)
  • The defence response (gradient damping) is targeted and proportional

Background — VFL, Label Inference, and Where Leakage Occurs

The VFL setup. Two parties — a passive party (Party A, no labels) and an active party (Party B, holds labels) — each own disjoint feature columns over the same set of samples. A server hosts the top model. During training, both parties compute local embeddings, forward them to the server, which computes the loss and back-propagates gradients to each party.

The Active Label Inference Attack. Party A (the attacker) modifies its local SGD update to amplify the gradient signal it receives from the server. Specifically, it uses MaliciousSGD: before updating its encoder, it scales the received gradient ∂ℒ/∂hA by a factor γ · r, where r = ‖gB‖/‖gA‖ is the inter-party gradient norm ratio (clamped to [1, 5]) and γ = 200.

gAmal = γ · clamp(‖gB‖/‖gA‖, 1, 5) · gA (MaliciousSGD)

This forces Party A's encoder to over-fit the gradient direction that encodes class boundaries, making hA far more class-discriminative than it would be under honest training. After training, Party A runs a semi-supervised learning step (MixMatch, 40 labelled samples) on recovered embeddings to infer the passive party's labels with high accuracy.

Key observation: The amplification changes how Party A's encoder learns, not what it predicts. Top-model accuracy on the joint task is essentially unchanged — the attack is invisible to standard monitoring. Attack label-inference accuracy: 94.95 ± 0.52% vs. benign baseline 83.11 ± 2.84%.
§3

The Fisher Class-Separability Criterion

The Fisher Linear Discriminant criterion (sometimes called the trace-ratio criterion) is a classical measure of how well a set of embeddings separates data points by class. It was introduced by R.A. Fisher in the context of linear discriminant analysis and asks: relative to within-class spread, how far apart are the class means?

J(X) = Tr(SB) / Tr(SW)

Tr(SB) = Σc nc · ‖μc − μ‖² ← between-class scatter
Tr(SW) = Σc Σx∈Cc ‖x − μc‖² ← within-class scatter (Fisher criterion)
Variables: X = embedding matrix; μc = mean embedding for class c; μ = global mean; nc = class c sample count; Cc = samples in class c. Higher J → embeddings are more class-discriminative.

Class 0 embeddings Class 1 embeddings Class mean

Why not cosine similarity or KL divergence? Cosine similarity between gradient vectors captures direction but not the structural shift in the embedding space. KL divergence requires fitting density models and does not naturally express the asymmetry between parties that we exploit. The Fisher criterion is: (1) interpretable as a direct measure of discriminative structure, (2) computable in closed form from embeddings, (3) naturally symmetric about zero when computed as a difference.

Note on terminology. The term "Fisher Divergence" also refers to a score-matching divergence DF(p‖q) = 𝔼p[‖∇log p − ∇log q‖²], used in energy-based generative models. Our method uses the Fisher Linear Discriminant criterion — a fundamentally different quantity — applied to embedding class-separability.

We define the Fisher Separability Divergence between the two parties as:

ΔF = J(hA) − J(hB) (detection signal)

This difference is near zero during benign training (both encoders develop similarly class-discriminative representations) and becomes significantly positive under attack (Party A's encoder is artificially forced to be far more class-discriminative than Party B's).

§4

Internal Mechanism — Step-by-Step

The defence is named Asymmetric Adaptive Perturbation (AAP). It operates as a monitoring layer that observes embeddings during normal VFL training and intervenes in the backward pass when the detection signal exceeds a threshold.

  1. Embed (forward pass) Each party runs its bottom model: raw features → embeddings hA, hB. These are forwarded to the server's top model. No change from standard VFL.
  2. Compute Fisher criteria After each epoch, the monitor collects hA and hB (and the corresponding labels from Party B's side). It computes JA = Tr(SBA) / Tr(SWA) and JB analogously for hB.
  3. Compute divergence ΔF ΔF = JA − JB. Under honest training: ΔF ≈ 0 (symmetric learning). Under attack: JA grows rapidly → ΔF ≫ 0.
  4. Threshold check If the current epoch ≥ burn-in period (b = 8 epochs) and ΔF > τ = 0.10, the defence activates. The burn-in prevents false triggering during the high-variance early phase of training.
  5. Gradient damping (AAP) The gradient sent to Party A's encoder is scaled by a damping factor λ:
λ = max(0, 1 − α · (ΔF − τ))

gAprotected = λ · gA (AAP damping)
Parameters: α = 1.0 (sensitivity), τ = 0.10 (threshold), b = 8 (burn-in epochs).
When ΔF = τ: λ = 1.0 (no change). When ΔF = τ + 1/α: λ = 0.0 (full suppression).
Figure 3 — AAP Damping Factor λ as a Function of ΔF
α = 1.0, τ = 0.10. The defence is proportional and saturates at zero.
Design rationale: The damping is proportional to the excess divergence (ΔF − τ). A large excess (deeply into attack territory) produces near-zero gradient transmission; a small excess (barely above threshold) produces minor attenuation. This preserves utility when the signal is ambiguous and strongly defends when the signal is clear.
§5

Why Fisher Divergence Detects the Attack

The attack works by engineering Party A's embedding space so that class information is maximally encoded there. To understand why ΔF is the right signal, consider what MaliciousSGD does geometrically.

During honest training, both parties' encoders develop representations that serve the joint prediction task. The top model decides how much each party's embedding contributes. Typically, Party A's and Party B's embeddings develop at roughly the same rate of class-discriminative structure: JA ≈ JB, so ΔF ≈ 0.

Under MaliciousSGD, Party A inflates its gradient by a factor up to γ · 5 = 1000×. This amplification forces Party A's encoder to minimise within-class variance and maximise between-class variance in hA, independent of what the top model requires. The effect on the Fisher criterion:

Tr(SWA) → decreases (within-class spread collapses — same-class embeddings cluster tightly)
Tr(SBA) → increases (between-class spread grows — class means pull apart)
∴ JA = Tr(SBA) / Tr(SWA) → grows rapidly

Party B is honest → JB stays stable
∴ ΔF = JA − JB → positive and growing (attack effect)
Figure 4 — Embedding Distribution Shift Under Attack (schematic)
3-class embedding spaces. Attack compresses within-class and separates between-class.

Why is ΔF stable under benign training? The top model's gradient signal naturally carries some class information to both encoders — this is what makes VFL work at all. But the signal is balanced: if Party A's embeddings become more class-discriminative, the top model will rely more on them, reducing the informative gradient signal to Party B and vice versa. This feedback loop keeps JA − JB approximately centred around zero.

The key invariant: In honest VFL, the class-discriminative structure in both parties' embeddings stays roughly symmetric — because neither party has an incentive to over-encode class boundaries beyond what the task requires. The attack breaks this symmetry, and ΔF measures exactly that breakage.

Threshold Selection — τ = 0.10

The threshold τ must separate benign-run ΔF values from attack-run values with low false positive and false negative rates. The chart below, derived from real experimental data, makes the separation visible.

Histograms of ΔF values across all training epochs. Benign run: 30 epochs. Attack run: 100 epochs. Threshold τ = 0.10 (purple).
Benign ΔF Attack ΔF τ = 0.10

Benign ΔF statistics (30 epochs)

  • Range: −0.084 to +0.056
  • Mean ≈ 0.008, Std ≈ 0.034
  • Never exceeds +0.056
  • All values below τ = 0.10
  • False positive rate: 0%

Attack ΔF statistics (100 epochs)

  • Range: −0.064 (epoch 0) to +1.43
  • Crosses τ = 0.10 at epoch 1
  • Stable above 0.40 from epoch 3 onward
  • All epochs ≥ burn-in (8) exceed τ
  • False negative rate: 0%

Sensitivity analysis. A higher τ would tolerate greater separability asymmetry before triggering — risking missed detections if the attack is subtle. A lower τ could trigger during the volatile early epochs (before burn-in). The burn-in of b = 8 epochs and τ = 0.10 form a complementary pair: the burn-in absorbs early noise; τ = 0.10 sits comfortably above the maximum observed benign value (0.056) while being far below the attack's stable operating range (≥ 0.40 after epoch 3).

Selection strategy: τ should be set above max(ΔFbenign) with a safety margin. Here: max benign = 0.056, τ = 0.10 (≈ 1.8× headroom). This can be calibrated on a small held-out benign validation run with negligible cost.

Alignment with the Active Label Inference Attack

The defence is precisely targeted because the detection signal is a direct consequence of the attack mechanism, not a proxy. The following side-by-side comparison shows where the defence intercepts the attack.

The defence is causally aligned: it monitors the exact geometric quantity that the attack inflates (JA), not a secondary effect. This means the defence cannot be evaded simply by slowing the attack down — any amplification sufficient to make label inference useful will increase JA, triggering damping.

Potential adaptive attack: An adversary who is aware of the detection mechanism could try to inflate JA and JB simultaneously (e.g., by also amplifying the gradient flowing to B's embedding if they control B). However, in the standard VFL threat model, Party B is honest and controls its own encoder — Party A cannot manipulate B's gradient without cooperation. This is a key structural protection.

Strengths & Weaknesses

Strengths

  • Causally aligned: The detection signal (ΔF) is the direct geometric effect of the attack, not a proxy
  • Zero false positives in all evaluated seeds under the chosen τ and burn-in
  • Low compute overhead: One extra scatter matrix computation per party per epoch — O(n·d·C) where C = number of classes
  • Proportional response: Damping scales with attack severity, minimising unnecessary utility loss
  • No access to labels needed on Party A's side — only hA and hB
  • Strong empirical results: 4/4 seeds PASS gate criterion; defended MC drops to below benign reference
  • Minimal utility cost: −1.91 pp test Top-1 accuracy

Weaknesses & Limitations

  • Requires label access to compute J: The monitor needs labels to compute between-class scatter — practical only if Party B (label holder) participates in computing JB
  • Assumes honest Party B: An adaptive attacker who controls both parties could jointly inflate JA and JB to mask the attack
  • Threshold calibration: τ should be set from benign validation; if benign separability is naturally high (certain datasets), the threshold may need adjustment
  • LR-schedule sensitivity: The epoch-50 LR decay causes a sudden jump in both curves — automated threshold adaptation would improve robustness
  • Evaluated on fixed γ = 200: A weaker attacker using smaller amplification might fall below τ; τ should be calibrated to the threat model
  • CIFAR-10/100 only so far: Generalisation to tabular or NLP VFL settings not yet validated
§11

Future Improvements & Extensions

Several directions could strengthen the theoretical contribution and address the limitations identified above:

Persistent Projection (PP)

Complement AAP with a persistent orthogonal projection that strips the gradient of its class-label-correlated component. PP and AAP target the same vulnerability from different angles: PP suppresses the direction, AAP suppresses the magnitude. A combined AAP+PP defence could be significantly more robust.

Adaptive Thresholding

Replace the fixed τ with an online estimate of the benign ΔF distribution (e.g., exponential moving average of JA − JB during burn-in), setting τ = mean + k·σ. This adapts to dataset-specific and LR-schedule-induced variation automatically.

Information Bottleneck

Explicitly minimise mutual information I(hA; Y) during training via a variational IB regulariser. This directly limits how much label information can be encoded in Party A's embeddings, providing a principled complement to the Fisher-based adaptive damping.

Mutual Information Minimisation

Directly penalise MI(hA; hB) using MINE or CLUB estimators. This weakens the coupling between embeddings that the attacker exploits, without requiring label access on Party A's side — addressing the main practical limitation.

Representation Orthogonalisation

Regularise Party A's encoder so that hA is approximately orthogonal to hB. This prevents the class-boundary information encoded in hB from being recoverable through hA's gradient, at the cost of some task-accuracy overhead.

Theoretical Bound

Derive a formal bound on label inference accuracy as a function of ΔF. This would establish a connection between the detection signal and the information-theoretic leakage, strengthening the theoretical contribution and justifying the proportional damping design.

Publication potential assessment: The core contribution — using the Fisher class-separability asymmetry as a detection signal and proportional gradient damping as a response — is well-motivated, novel, and empirically validated. The 4/4-seed gate result and near-zero utility cost strengthen the argument. Multi-dataset validation (tabular: Criteo/Census; text: Yahoo Answers) and the PP bug-fix follow-up experiments are the primary remaining milestones before submission to a top-tier venue (USENIX Security, IEEE S&P, or NeurIPS/ICML privacy workshop).
